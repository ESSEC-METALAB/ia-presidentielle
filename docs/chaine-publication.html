Chaîne de Publication

How the Observatoire works, from a candidate's speech to a line on a public page.

01 The idea

A candidate says something about artificial intelligence. It might be in a fifty page programme, a speech filmed at a tech conference, a post on X, or a vote in parliament. Six months later nobody can find it, and three newspapers have reported three different numbers.

The Observatoire fixes one narrow thing. It watches a fixed list of places where candidates speak, pulls out what they said about AI, and publishes each position with the exact quote, the date and a link. In French and in English.

You read it two ways. Le fil is a feed, newest first, for what changed this week. La grille is a table, candidates down the side and six policy themes across the top, for comparing them side by side.

The hard part is not collecting the text. It is that an institution's name is on the result. So every rule below exists for one reason, which is that a reader has to be able to check anything we publish without taking our word for it.

02 The words we use

Six words that come up on every page below. Two minutes here saves confusion later.

DocumentA piece of text we collected and kept. A programme, an article, the subtitles of a video.
LeadSomething we noticed but will not publish from. A headline saying a candidate spoke about AI. It is a question for the editor, not an answer.
PositionOne thing a candidate said. A quote, a theme, a date and a link. This is what appears on the site.
TierWhose words they are. 1 the candidate himself, 2 his party, 3 a journalist reporting on him. Not who hosts the page.
AxisOne of six policy themes: regulation, sovereignty, work, public services, surveillance, funding. Every claim gets exactly one.
Le fil · La grilleThe two views of the same claims. A feed and a comparison table.

03 Sources

What matters is whose words they are, not which website they sit on. A speech is the candidate's own words wherever it is published. Only his words and his party's become positions. A journalist writing about him becomes a lead instead, which is a question for the editor rather than something we publish. A press article is read once and filed under whichever candidate it names, by looking for the name. We never ask the model who was being quoted.

06 Anatomy of a claim

Everything we publish keeps three voices apart and shows the reader which is which. That separation is the whole argument for trusting it.

His words
« Pour les Français, je propose un agent IA administratif unique, « Marianne », capable de simplifier toutes leurs démarches. »
What he committed to
Propose un agent conversationnel unique, « Marianne », pour l'ensemble des démarches administratives.
What it means
Guichet unique piloté par IA. Le périmètre des démarches couvertes n'est pas précisé.
The receipt
theme: public services · his party's site · 7 July 2026 · archived copy kept
The candidate
Exactly as he said it. Never edited, corrected or translated. Kept short, because French law allows quoting briefly when you say where it came from.
Extraction
One plain sentence saying what he committed to. It describes and never grades. Words of judgement are rejected automatically.
Metalab
What the position means in practice. This is where the institute adds something, and it carries a label so no reader mistakes it for his words.
Recorded automatically
Where it came from, the date, and a link to an archived copy taken the day we read it. Pages get edited and deleted, so without that copy a quote becomes unprovable.

07 Approaches we rejected

Both were sensible suggestions. Neither survives what this publication has to promise a reader.

Sentiment analysis and EDA

The text is already concreteCandidates give real figures and named plans. Billions of euros, numbers of civil servants, a scheme with a name. There is nothing to infer. You copy what is written.
A score is a judgementSentiment produces a number. A number puts candidates in an order. An institution publishing an order of candidates during an election is the one thing everything here exists to avoid.
It cannot be checkedA quote either appears in the source or it does not, and anyone can look. A score has no equivalent test, so a reader would simply have to trust us.

Counting has one use, and it stays internal. Seeing which candidate and which theme we have little on tells the editor where to look next. It is never published as a finding.

A vision model reading the video

We have not tried it, so we cannot tell you what it would cost. Any figure we gave would be arithmetic rather than experience. None of the reasons below depend on the answer.

It answers a different questionIt tells you who is on screen. We need to know who is talking. French television cuts to the interviewer while the candidate is still speaking, and uses split screens and stock footage. Being on screen usually means you are speaking. Usually is not good enough when the whole product is knowing who said what.
Wrong sense entirelyTelling voices apart is a sound problem, not a picture problem. Tools exist that separate speakers from the audio. That is the right instrument for the job.
It cannot produce the evidenceA quote has to appear word for word in the source. A model looking at pictures describes what it saw, it does not transcribe. So you would still need the subtitles anyway.
It breaks replayHow often you look at the video is a choice. Look at different moments and you get a different answer. Everything here is built so a result can be traced back and repeated.
It saves the reviewer nothingSomeone still confirms before anything is published, and the subtitles already give the exact second, reliably and for nothing.

Where it would genuinely earn its place. Text shown on screen and never spoken, such as a figure that appears only on a slide. And pages of a programme that are charts rather than words. That is a handful of images, not thousands, and it is cheap. Worth doing. Just not for working out who spoke.
